1. The Map

Purpose: to provide information on the existence, location, and distance between ground features. Definition: a geographic representation of the earth's surface drawn to scale as seen from above.

A map shows location of ground objects, populated areas, routes of travel, communication lines, extent of vegetation cover, and elevation/relief.

Care: protect from water, mud, and tearing. Mark with light lines that erase cleanly. If trimming, don't cut marginal information. Maps hold tactical information — friendly positions and friendly supply points.

The Five Basic Colors

ColorRepresents
BlackMajority of cultural/man-made features — buildings, bridges, roads not shown in red
RedMain roads, built-up areas, special features such as dangerous or restricted areas
BlueWater — lakes, rivers, swamps, streams
GreenVegetation — woods, orchards
Red BrownAll landforms — contours, fills, cuts

Other colors may appear for special information; these are explained in the marginal information.

Marginal Information

  • Sheet Name — center of the upper margin and the lower right margin
  • Contour Interval — center lower margin; vertical distance between adjacent contour lines
  • Grid Box — basic instruction on reading grids
  • Declination Diagram — lower margin; angular relationship of true, grid, and magnetic north
  • Legend — lower left margin; identifies symbols. Also holds Sheet Name, Sheet Number, Series Name, Edition Number, Index to Boundaries, Index to Adjoining Sheets, Series Number
  • Bar Scale — center bottom below the map face; meters, yards, statute miles, nautical miles. Zero is not at the end of the scale

The Three Norths

  • True North — a line from any position connecting at the North Pole. All lines of longitude are true north lines.
  • Magnetic North — direction to the North Magnetic Pole as shown by the compass needle. Located in Canada at Hudson Bay.
  • Grid North — established by the vertical grid lines on the map. Variation from true north is due to curvature of the earth.
GM Angle — used to convert magnetic azimuth to grid azimuth and vice versa. Grid azimuth is measured from grid north with a protractor. Magnetic azimuth is taken from a compass and measured from magnetic north.

2. Measuring Distance

Straight-line

  • Lay a straight strip of paper so the edge touches the center of both points.
  • Tick the paper at each point.
  • Lay the strip along the bar scale matching your unit of measure.
  • Place the right tick on the largest full unit on the primary scale (right of zero); the remainder falls on the extension scale (left of zero).

Curved or irregular

  • Tick near one end of the irregular line.
  • Align the strip along the center of the first straight portion.
  • Tick the other end of that portion on both strip and map.
  • Holding both ticks together, pivot the strip at the second tick until the next straight portion aligns.
  • Continue, then read the total on the bar scale.

3. Protractor

  • All protractors divide the circle into units of angular measure, with a scale around the outer edge and an index mark.
  • The index mark is the center of the protractor circle from which all directions are measured.
  • The military protractor has two scales: degrees inner, mils outer. It represents the azimuth circle.
  • Degree scale graduated 0°–360°; each tick = one degree. The line from 0° to 180° is the base line. Where the base line crosses the horizontal line between 90° and 270° is the index/center.
  • In use, the base line is always parallel to a north-south grid line; 0°/360° toward the top (north) of the map, 90° to the right.

4. The Grid System

A network of lines forming squares on the face of the map, like city blocks. Names are all numbers; every tenth line is heavier. Grid line numbers appear within the map on the line itself.

DigitsIdentifies
4-digit1,000-meter grid square
6-digit100-meter grid square
8-digit10-meter grid square
Rule of map reading: READ RIGHT AND UP.

5. Compass Terms and Concepts

Azimuth — an angle measured in a clockwise direction from a north base line.

  • East = 90° · South = 180° · West = 270° · North = 360° or 0°
  • The point the azimuth originates from is imagined as the center of the azimuth circle.

Obtaining a grid azimuth

  • Draw a line connecting the two points — A is your present location, B your destination.
  • Place the protractor index on point A.
  • Ensure the 0°/180° base line is parallel with the vertical grid lines.
  • Read the azimuth from the degree (inside) scale — that is the grid azimuth from A to B.
Back azimuth — the reverse direction of a forward azimuth, like doing an about face. If the azimuth is less than 180°, add 180°. If it is 180° or more, subtract 180°.

6. Lensatic Compass

The primary instrument used to determine and maintain direction during land navigation.

Parts (14)

Thumb loopGraduated straight edge
Short luminous lineLens
Luminous sighting dotsFixed index line
Luminous magnetic arrow ("Magnetic North")Bezel ring
Sighting slotCover
Sighting wireRear sight
Floating dial (mils and degrees)Base

Precautions

  • Handle with care — the dial is delicately balanced and shock can damage it.
  • Never take a reading near visible masses of metal or electrical circuits.
  • In cold weather carry it in its carrier outside your outer layer; carried against the body it fogs when exposed to cold air.

Two methods of holding

Compass-to-cheek — recommended for determining the azimuth to a distant object.

  • Raise the cover (sighting wire) straight up; raise the lens about 45° above the compass glass.
  • Turn the thumb loop all the way down, thumb through it, loose fist under the compass with the other hand, raise to eye level.
  • Look through the sighting slot and center the sighting wire in it.
  • Keep the compass level and sights aligned; rotate your entire body until the sighting wire is on the object.
  • Glance down through the lens and read the magnetic azimuth under the fixed index line.

Center-hold — recommended for a predetermined azimuth, day or night.

  • No need to remove helmet, weapon, grenades, or magazines so long as they are not near the compass.
  • Open the cover until it forms a straight edge; pull the eyepiece to the rearmost position.
  • Thumb through the thumb loop, steady base with remaining fingers, other hand forms a solid base.
  • Turn your entire body toward the object, point the cover at it, and read the azimuth beneath the black index line.

Night use

  • All luminous features are used. The top glass (bezel ring) rotates; each click = 3 degrees.
  • 30 clicks counterclockwise = 90 degrees.
  • Using center-hold, rotate body and compass until the magnetic north-seeking arrow sits directly under the short luminous line.
  • March by keeping the arrow and short luminous line aligned, following the luminous dots on the cover.

7. Orientation of a Map

A map is oriented when north and south on the map correspond to north and south on the ground.

With a compass

  • Map horizontal; compass straight edge parallel to a north-south grid line, cover pointing toward the top of the map.
  • This places the black index line parallel to grid north; the needle points to magnetic north, forming a declination diagram on the compass face.
  • Rotate map and compass together until that relationship matches the declination diagram in the map margin.
  • Magnetic north left of grid north: compass reading equals the GM angle. Magnetic north right of grid north: compass reading equals 360 minus the GM angle.

Without a compass — terrain association

  • Find linear features common to both map and ground: roads, railroads, fence lines, power lines.
  • Align the feature on the map with the same feature on the ground.
  • Check with two or more features to prevent reversal of direction.

8. Determining Location by Map and Compass

  • Inspection and estimation — usually easiest; carefully survey road systems and topographic features in the immediate vicinity.
  • Orient the map to the ground — identify a prominent characteristic (road, junction, bridge, stream) visible on the ground and unmistakable on the map.

9. 90-Degree Offset Method

Used to bypass enemy positions or obstacles and stay oriented — detour by moving at right angles for specified distances.

RALS — Right Add 90 degrees; Left Subtract 90 degrees.

10. Pace Count

  • Keeps a record of ground distance; recorded in 100-meter increments.
  • Step off with the left foot and count every time it hits the deck.
  • Record each 100-meter increment by tying a knot in a rope or piece of string.
Formula: distance ÷ 100 × pace count.
Example: 500 m with a 65-pace count → 500 ÷ 100 × 65 = 325 paces.

11. Elevation and Relief

Contour lines indicate elevation and relief. Each is an imaginary line along which all points are at the same elevation above sea level. The contour interval is given in the marginal information. On most maps contours are printed red-brown starting at zero elevation. Every fifth contour line is heavier — an index contour line — and carries the elevation.

  • Line spacing indicates the nature of the slope, which has military significance.
  • The closer the contour lines, the steeper the terrain.

The Seven Natural (Land) Formations

FeatureDescription / contour signature
HillA point or small area of high ground; closed concentric contours
ValleyStream course with at least a limited extent of level ground bordered by higher ground; U-shaped contours, the curve of the crossing always points up (upstream)
DrawA less developed stream with essentially no level ground and little or no maneuver room; ground slopes up on each side and toward the head; V-shaped contours with the point toward the head of the draw
RidgeA line of high ground with minor variations along its crest; the crest is higher than the ground on both sides
SaddleA dip or low point along the crest of a ridge; not necessarily between two hilltops — may be a dip or break along an otherwise level ridge crest
DepressionA low point or sinkhole surrounded on all sides by higher ground
CliffA vertical or near-vertical slope; too steep to show at the contour interval, so shown by a ticked (carrying) contour line — ticks always point toward lower ground
